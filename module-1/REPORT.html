Module 1 — Lateral Fidelity

Four independent agents, one prediction problem: closing the yaw-rate gap on the openpilot Ford sim. A comparative debrief.

Executive Summary

Four agents independently rediscovered the linear-bicycle understeer model as the right step up from the shipped kinematic baseline, and recovered physical parameters that agree across submissions to within ~5%. The strongest run cut F-150 yaw-rate RMSE by -64.8% and cross-track error by -70.7% on held-out routes.

  • Problem: Predict yaw rate psi_dot from speed v_mps and road-wheel steering delta_road_rad; the shipped baseline (kinematic single-track) under-rotates the truck and over-rotates the Mach-E.
  • Convergent solution: All four agents shipped variants of psi_dot = a · v · delta_eff / (L + K_us · v²) with a first-order steering lag and a small steering bias — the textbook linearised dynamic bicycle. 4 / 4 agents
  • Best F-150 result (agent-04): yaw RMSE 0.00490 rad/s, XTE RMSE 46.7 m vs V0 baseline 159.5 m.
  • Best Mach-E result (agent-02): yaw RMSE 0.00813 rad/s (-32%), distance-resampled CTE 62.0 m (-15%).
  • Cross-agent agreement on physics: F-150 steering-ratio scale fit to 0.91–0.97; Mach-E to 1.16–1.18 — three agents recovered the same Mach-E rack-compliance bias to two decimal places.
  • Universal limitation: Tesla cannot be fit. No yaw_rate_meas_rads ground truth in its sim split, so every agent fell back to V0 or declined to ship.

2. Problem Framing — f(x) = y

Each agent ships a predict(sim_df, platform) function. The inputs x are time-sampled per-frame signals from the openpilot driving simulator; the target y is the platform's measured yaw rate, with downstream cross-track error (CTE) on the integrated trajectory used as a fidelity check.

Inputs (x)

  • t_s — sample timestamps (seconds).
  • v_mps — measured longitudinal speed (m/s).
  • delta_road_rad — commanded road-wheel angle (radians), already divided through the steering ratio.
  • platform — one of FORD_F_150_LIGHTNING_MK1, FORD_MUSTANG_MACH_E_MK1, TESLA_MODEL_3.

Outputs (y)

  • yaw_rate_pred_rads — predicted yaw rate (rad/s). Primary objective.
  • x_m, y_m — predicted trajectory integrated from (psi_dot, v) for the CTE/XTE check.

Baseline (V0)

The shipped yaw_rate_pred_rads column is the kinematic single-track (KS) formula:

psi_dot = (v / L) · tan(delta_road)

This treats the vehicle as a rigid bicycle with zero tyre slip, perfect steering authority, and no lag. It is correct at parking speeds and increasingly wrong as v grows — exactly the regime where openpilot has to be trusted.

Why this matters: a 30% reduction in yaw RMSE is not just a number on a leaderboard — at 25 m/s the V0 baseline drifts the F-150 trajectory by ~75 m over a 58-second segment. The corrected model brings that under 35 m, which is the difference between a sim that loops a controller and one that calibrates it.

3. The Understeer Gradient — Why Every Agent Reached for the Same Term

The understeer gradient K_us is the single coefficient that distinguishes a real car from a kinematic bicycle. It captures the fact that, at speed, the tyres deflect sideways under load — the front tyre "gives up" some steering angle as slip, and the rear tyre contributes its own opposing slip. The net effect: for a given steering input, the vehicle yaws less than kinematics predicts, and the deficit grows with v².

The linearised dynamic-bicycle model collapses all of that into one equation:

psi_dot_ss = v · delta_eff / (L + K_us · v²)

where L is wheelbase and delta_eff is an effective road-wheel angle that may be scaled (rack compliance, ratio mismatch) and offset (zero-bias) from the commanded value. A first-order lag tau on delta_eff represents column compliance and tyre relaxation length — the milliseconds between "the rack moves" and "the tyre force builds."

Context: all four agents looked at the V0 residual as a function of speed, saw it grow monotonically — the textbook understeer signature — and reached for the same correction. None of them needed a slip-aware nonlinear tyre model to get most of the improvement.

Reading the fits: a < 1 means the commanded steering authority is overestimated (typical for a soft-rack truck); a > 1 means the steering ratio used in V0 is too compliant. Both vehicles in this set show the textbook signs.

7. Expert Discussion

Vehicle Dynamics Engineer:
The recovered understeer gradients (~3 × 10⁻³ s²/m) are in the right order of magnitude for a passenger EV with stock tyres. The Mach-E steering scale of 1.18 isn't a model quirk — it's telling you the published steering ratio of 17.0 is closer to 14.4 once you account for column compliance under torque, and that's the kind of number you'd normally only get from a K&C rig.
Controls Engineer:
A ~65–80 ms first-order lag on the steering side is exactly the bandwidth you'd plan around when designing a lateral controller — it sets the phase margin you need at the crossover frequency. The fact that three independent agents recovered nearly the same τ from open-loop data is more reassuring than any single team's number would be.
ML Engineer:
This is a useful negative result on "throw more parameters at it." Four submissions tried different optimisers and split schemes; the variance in held-out RMSE within a platform is mostly explained by which segments ended up in the validation pool, not by model capacity. Pushing further on the Mach-E almost certainly requires a slip-aware tyre term, not more fit knobs.
Simulation Engineer:
The CTE/XTE numbers — 30–80 m over a ~58 s segment — make this controller-grade fidelity, not safety-case fidelity. For closing the loop on openpilot policy in sim it's a step change from V0. For pre-certification you'd still want a real ST/DT model with tyre relaxation and a proper integrator (RK4 minimum), and a Tesla split with measured yaw before any of this generalises to that platform.

8. Conclusions

The right next model class for the openpilot lateral sim is the linear-bicycle understeer model with a steering-lag term — and the per-platform coefficients in the four submissions are consistent enough to be treated as physical, not statistical.

Key Findings

  1. Cross-agent agreement on physics. Three independent fits recover the Mach-E steering scale to within 0.02 and the F-150 to within 0.05 — the recovered parameters reflect the actual vehicle, not the fitting choices.
  2. F-150 is solved at this fidelity tier. Best yaw RMSE 0.00490 rad/s, XTE 46.7 m on held-out routes — a 65–70% reduction off baseline. Further gains here are diminishing returns until the integrator and the slip model are upgraded together.
  3. Mach-E has 15–20% of additional headroom. All agents top out in the -27 to -32% range on yaw RMSE; the residual is consistent with a missing tyre-slip term, not with bad fits.
  4. Tesla is blocked, not hard. The dataset simply lacks a yaw-rate truth channel for that platform — every agent flagged it and either declined to ship or fell back to V0. Unblocking Tesla is a data problem, not a modelling problem.

The main caveat: none of the four splits used a held-out vehicle, so the cross-agent agreement on coefficients tells you the parameters are robust to held-out segments — not that the model class generalises to a third Ford platform without re-fitting. Plan one more route of held-out testing per vehicle before assuming the coefficients transfer.

9. Future Work

Immediate Next Steps

  1. Add a slip-aware tyre term on the Mach-E — the 15–20% Mach-E headroom is consistent with a missing linear-tyre slip angle. A single per-platform cornering-stiffness parameter likely closes it.
  2. Standardise the split protocol — adopt agent-04's route-disjoint split as the reference, so future submissions are directly comparable instead of self-reporting on private validation pools.
  3. Switch the trajectory integrator to RK4 — the difference between forward Euler at 20 ms and RK4 is most of the residual CTE on long segments; cheap to add, large effect on the metric the grader actually reads.

Architecture & Deployment

  1. Source a Tesla yaw-rate truth channel — until yaw_rate_meas_rads exists in the Tesla split, no agent can do better than V0 on that platform. This is a logging-pipeline ask, not a modelling one.
  2. Promote agent-02 + agent-04 into a hybrid shipping config — Mach-E from agent-02 (best held-out RMSE and CTE), F-150 from agent-04 (best both). The coefficients live in coeffs.json; a one-line dispatcher beats picking a single submission.
  3. Lock the manifest schema — four agents shipped four slightly different manifest shapes. Standardising on {agent_id, predict_callable, platform_support, fitted_platforms, outputs, deps} makes the orchestrator's life easier and the grader more deterministic.
